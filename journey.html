Hazel's Wearcast

How I built a weather dress-up app in two weeks, what kept breaking, and how I got it working.

What it does

Wearcast reads the hourly forecast and my plans for the day (the occasion, the hours I'll be out, and how easily I get cold). It picks one complete outfit from a fixed 57-item wardrobe, dresses a pixel-art version of me in it, and explains the choice.

Who did what

I wrote the spec, made every design decision, and tested everything. Claude Code wrote the code, and ChatGPT's image generator drew the art.

The hard part

I expected the weather logic to be hard. Getting AI-drawn clothes to line up on one body was much harder, and it took four days of small fixes.

1Jun 5 to 6

The plan

I'm bad at reading the weather. If a forecast says 14 °C with wind, I still can't tell whether that means a hoodie or a coat. The answer also changes with my plans: hanging out with friends, working out, going to class, and going to the office all need different clothes. I wanted an app that would decide for me based on the weather and on what I'm doing that day.

Before writing any code I made two documents. The first was a 13-section PRD addressed to Claude Code. It set the rule order (temperature decides warmth, occasion picks the style, weather adds accessories) and the main choices:

Decisions
  • Four occasions to choose from (Play, Active, School, Work), each with its own style rules
  • Open-Meteo for weather: free, no key, callable from the browser
  • Use "feels like" temperature, not the raw number
  • Dress for the coldest hour of the outing and point out layers that can come off later
  • A body-type setting, because I get cold more easily than most people. On cold-sensitive, the app treats the day as 3 °C colder than the forecast, so I end up dressed warmer. Heat-sensitive does the opposite.

The second was an asset list for the image generator: 60 images, one base character plus 59 clothes. Its main rule was that every sprite use the same canvas and the same position as the base character, so the clothes could simply be stacked.

What went wrong

The first batch came back at 512×640 with only ten tops, and the character looked nothing like what I wanted.

How I got past it

I regenerated, and by that morning I had the full set of 60 sprites at 437×1211. The "same canvas, same position" rule would still come back to bite me.

2Jun 6

The first build

Claude Code built version 1 from the PRD in one day. The "brain" was a lookup table of temperature bands (28 °C and up, 23 to 27, and so on), each with suitable tops, bottoms, and jackets. The character is a stack of see-through PNGs drawn in a set order, and each piece flies in from its closet tile.

What went wrong
  • "Use my location" didn't work when I opened the file directly.
  • My edits didn't show up after reloading.
  • Each hat sprite has its own face drawn in, at a different height, so a hat added a second pair of eyes in the wrong place.
How I got past it
  • Browsers only allow location on localhost or HTTPS, so I added serve.py, a tiny local server.
  • The browser was caching old files. serve.py tells it not to.
  • I measured the eye height in every sprite (15.8% of the way down on the base, 25.0% on the cap and beanie, 34.3% on the bucket hat) and shifted each hat by the difference.

I also added two things the PRD didn't have: sleep mode, which keeps her in pajamas if the outing starts between 11 PM and 5 AM, and Coordinate your own, which lets you dress her by hand.

3Jun 7 to 10

Making the clothes fit

This was the hardest part of the project. It took four days and the two longest sessions I had with Claude (46 and 145 back-and-forth turns). The generator had been told to put every item in the same place on the same canvas, and it didn't.

What went wrong
  • Jackets were drawn about 1.77× too small.
  • Waistbands sat at 33% of the canvas. Her real waist, which I marked, is at 46%.
  • Long pants and leggings were too short.
  • Each pair of shoes sat too close together to land under her feet.
  • The scarf covered her mouth and the gloves missed her hands.
  • Tights were drawn on top of the shoes.
  • Leggings should tuck into boots and jeans should cover them, but one fixed layer order can't do both.
  • Claude kept leaving my preview zoomed out to 50% after taking screenshots.
How I got past it

Instead of redrawing art, I added a fit table in code: a scale, shift, and rotation for each clothing type, with per-item overrides. My rule was that position is handled in code, and an image only gets edited for a left/right gap.

  • All jackets share one scale, anchored at the shoulders.
  • All tops share the tank top's fit, plus small tweaks like "shrink the polo 3%".
  • Every bottom is pinned to the 46% waistline and stretched to its hem with one formula. Shorts keep their shape so the strawberry print doesn't squish.
  • Shoes were widened to match the sandals' spacing. The loafers alone took five rounds.
  • Tights moved under the shoes, and leggings got a tuckable flag so boots go over them.
  • Layer order and fly-in order now come from the same sort, so they can't disagree.
  • Claude saved a standing rule to reset the zoom after screenshots.

I backed up every image before touching it. There are 35 backup files in the sprite folder now, which is a fair count of how many tries this took.

// bottoms: drop the waistband onto the 46% line
sy = (hem% − 46) / (spriteBottom% − spriteTop%)

I also swapped in a cleaner ChatGPT redraw of the same character. I removed its background and scaled it so her head and feet sat on the same pixels as before, and every outfit still fit without retuning.

4By Jun 10

A smarter engine that reads your plans

This is the heart of Wearcast. Version 1 only looked at the temperature: it took the first suitable item in a band and couldn't say why one outfit beat another. I wanted it to dress me for what I'm actually doing that day, so I designed a new engine in LOGIC.md before any of it was coded.

What it reads about your day

Occasion

Sets how active you'll be and what's allowed. Active dresses lighter because moving makes heat. Work follows a dress code.

Hours you'll be out

It dresses you for the coldest hour, then tells you which layer can come off once it warms up.

Body type

I get cold easily, so on cold-sensitive it treats the day as 3 °C colder and dresses me warmer.

Weather details

Rain adds an umbrella or rain boots, strong sun adds sunglasses and a hat, and cold wind adds a scarf and gloves.

How it picks the outfit

  1. Set a warmth target. It measures warmth in clo, the real unit of clothing insulation (1 clo is about a business suit at 21 °C). She needs about 0.15 clo at 30 °C, 1.0 at 12 °C, and 1.8 at 0 °C.
  2. Adjust for the occasion. Active gets a lower target, but the discount is capped, so it never suggests a t-shirt at −5 °C for a jog.
  3. Try every outfit. It builds every complete outfit allowed for the weather and occasion (a few hundred), adds up the clo, and picks the one closest to the target. Outfits with a layer you can take off later rank higher.
  4. Score and explain it. It rates the outfit out of 100 against the best realistic fabrics and shows its reasoning.

Same day, different plans

Here's one 14 °C windy day in Waterloo (feels like 10 to 12 °C). The weather stays exactly the same, and only the occasion changes.

  • Play: sweatshirt, jeans, and sneakers
  • Active: sweatshirt, joggers, and sneakers
  • Work: light knit, slacks, and loafers

All three get a light puffer for the chilly morning.

Same day, but I'm cold

Switching the body type to cold-sensitive makes the engine treat that same day as 3 °C colder. For Play, the sweatshirt and jeans become a heavy knit, fleece-lined pants, a coat, and boots.

The old lookup table stayed as a filter for what's allowed. I also added a test mode: adding ?dev to the address runs rules like "no puffer above 10 °C" and "a dress always replaces the top and bottom".

5Jun 12

Bugs I found by using it

Two days later I planned a real day out: Las Vegas on June 27, 9 AM to 6 PM, feels-like 35 to 43 °C, wind 39 km/h.

A beanie in the desert

Hazel showed up in a tank top, shorts, sandals, sunglasses and a parasol, plus a beanie, a scarf and gloves.

Why

Wind of 30 km/h or more added winter gear at any temperature. But "feels like" already includes wind chill, so wind was being counted twice. My tests missed it because every one of them used zero wind.

How I fixed it

Wind now adds warm gear only when it's also cold. I added a test with strong wind at 40, 30, 24, 18 and 13 °C, then had every other accessory rule checked for the same mistake. Wind was the only one. The Vegas score went from 82 to 96.

const coldWind = wind >= 30 && adjFeelsMin <= 10;
if (veryCold || coldWind) headwear = 'hat_beanie';

The wind still gets a warning, just in the weather popup ("hold onto your hat") instead of as a beanie.

A broken image above her head

A broken-image icon labeled "Face (over outerwear)" appeared above her head with coats and in sleep mode.

Why

An old layer that redrew her face over high collars was still being added, but its image file no longer existed. It was probably left behind when the base was redrawn.

How I fixed it

The new base didn't need it, so I had it removed everywhere: the item, its layer, its fit values, the code that added it, and its docs.

"Do my changes go to GitHub automatically?"

They don't. Nothing leaves my computer until it's committed and pushed, so I committed and pushed both fixes that night.

6Jun 12 to 13

Teaching it about occasions

Midway through this work I hit my Claude usage limit and had to wait for it to reset, and the conversation got so long it had to be compacted. I picked it back up and shipped all three features in one commit that evening.

A Work dress code

Work means long pants only, no skirts or dresses. It also bans tank tops, sweatshirts, hoodies, shorts, jeans, joggers, short leggings, windbreakers, sandals, rain boots and sunglasses. Shoes are loafers, or boots when it's cold or wet.

The conflict

In extreme heat, no long pants count as weather-appropriate under the engine's own rules.

My call

The dress code wins. The engine picks the lightest trousers (slacks) and suggests linen. Tests check every temperature from 42 °C down to −10 °C in calm, sunny and rainy weather, and nothing banned ever appears.

"You may need to go shopping"

If even the best fabrics can't get an outfit to 90 points, the closet has no good answer, and a popup says so. I found my first message boring, compared four funnier drafts, and chose "It'll do… for now".

More than one look

Pressing Start again for the same day used to show the same outfit. Now it cycles through up to five different looks, each scoring 95 or higher, with a "Look 2 of 5" hint. Changing any input starts over.

7Jun 13 to 17

Polish

What went wrong
  • The search box opened with "Seoul" or "Vancouver" already filled in. Seoul was test data Claude saved, and the app also remembered the last city.
  • Claude couldn't use the app icon I pasted into the chat.
  • The chats from June 6 to 10 had been deleted automatically, and that history was gone.
How I got past it
  • Every visit now starts with an empty search box. Body type and occasion are still remembered.
  • I saved the pink hanger image into the project folder, and it became the browser tab icon and the home-screen icon.
  • I started writing a hand-off note at the end of long sessions, so the next one can pick up where I left off.

No pull request

I also skipped making a pull request, since everything was already on main and a PR would have meant rewriting history.

★What I took away

Lessons

  1. A written rule still needs checking."Same canvas, same position" was in the asset list and the generator ignored it. Measuring and correcting in code worked better than asking for redraws.
  2. Tests only catch what they test.The beanie bug slipped through because every test had zero wind. Now each fix comes with the test that would have caught it.
  3. Watch for double-counting."Feels like" already includes wind. Counting it again dressed her for a blizzard in the desert.
  4. Plan on paper first.The PRD made the first build fast, and LOGIC.md let me design the new engine before writing code.
  5. Saving to GitHub is a step I take.Nothing gets there until I commit and push.
  6. Write things down before they disappear.A hand-off note takes a minute. Rebuilding lost history took far longer.

Where it stands (September 2026)

  • Live on GitHub Pages, with 5 commits on main.
  • 57 wardrobe items, 60 sprites (plus 35 backups), and about 2,360 lines of JavaScript.
